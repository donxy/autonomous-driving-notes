## Prologue: The 2018 Tempe crash — the industry pays full price for safety culture for the first time

- **On the night of March 18, 2018**, an Uber test vehicle (a Volvo XC90) in autonomous driving mode struck and killed Elaine Herzberg (49 years old), who was pushing a bicycle across the road, in Tempe, Arizona — the first case of a pedestrian killed by an autonomous driving system.
- In November 2019 the NTSB adopted its final report by 3–0: the **probable cause** was that the safety driver was visually distracted throughout by looking at a phone and did not monitor the road; the **contributing factors** were Uber's inadequate safety risk assessment procedures, ineffective oversight of safety drivers, and the absence of mechanisms to address "automation complacency" — it found a safety culture problem, not just one person's problem. In addition, the system did not correctly identify the pedestrian, and the vehicle's original automatic emergency braking had been disabled.
- The judicial outcome was asymmetric: in 2019 Arizona prosecutors found Uber the company bore no criminal liability, and the company settled quickly with the deceased's family; the safety driver Rafaela Vasquez was indicted for negligent homicide in 2020, and in July 2023 pleaded guilty to the reduced charge of "endangerment", sentenced to 3 years of supervised probation, with no imprisonment.
- After the crash Uber suspended all road testing for several months, and ATG's safety credibility and internal standing were damaged together — two years later it was sold off as a whole.

Sources: [NTSB report highlights (2019-11)](https://www.designdevelopmenttoday.com/industries/automotive/news/21102489/ntsb-blames-distracted-driver-in-fatal-uber-crash); [Safety driver guilty-plea report (2023-07)](https://www.mtpr.org/2023-07-28/backup-driver-of-an-autonomous-uber-pleads-guilty-to-endangerment-in-pedestrian-death)

## The ride-hailing duo exit: ATG sold to Aurora, Level 5 sold to Toyota

- **Uber ATG → Aurora (announced December 7, 2020)**: Aurora acquires Uber's autonomous driving division ATG (about 1,200 employees) for no cash; Uber invests $400 million back into Aurora, holds about 26%, and its CEO joins Aurora's board. ATG's implied valuation in this deal was about $4 billion, already down by nearly half compared with the $7.25 billion valuation at its 2019 financing.
- **Lyft Level 5 → Woven Planet (signed April 26, 2021)**: Toyota subsidiary Woven Planet acquires Lyft's autonomous driving division for about $550 million in cash ($200 million prepaid + $350 million over five years), with the team of more than 300 people joining as a whole; Lyft ends nearly four years of in-house development and switches to the route of "partnering with external autonomous driving companies to get onto the platform".
- The common background to both deals: under earnings pressure, the first things ride-hailing companies cut are the in-house projects with the longest payback periods and the highest cash burn — ATG and Level 5 were not disproven as technology routes; their owners could no longer wait.

Sources: [TechCrunch: Uber sells ATG (2020-12-07)](https://techcrunch.com/2020/12/07/uber-sells-self-driving-unit-uber-atg-in-deal-that-will-push-auroras-valuation-to-10b/); [Toyota official: Woven Planet acquires Level 5 (2021-04)](https://global.toyota/en/newsroom/corporate/35244091.html)

## The disillusionment of the automaker joint venture: Argo AI shuts down

- Argo AI was a joint venture between Ford and Volkswagen: Ford first invested $1 billion in 2017; in the Volkswagen deal announced in July 2019 and closed in 2020, Volkswagen put in $2.6 billion ($1 billion in cash + its European autonomous driving subsidiary AID contributed at a value of $1.6 billion), and spent a further $500 million buying shares from Ford.
- **On October 26, 2022, Argo announced its shutdown**, with some of its about 2,000 employees transferring into Ford and Volkswagen; the direct cause of death was its failure to attract new external investors to take over. Ford recorded a **$2.7 billion** non-cash pre-tax impairment for this, with a net loss of $827 million that quarter.
- Ford stated its turn very plainly: autonomous driving capital expenditure shifts from L4 to in-house L2+/L3 — assisted driving that can be sold to consumers in the near term takes priority over driverless taxis that might make money at some unknown time. On the Volkswagen side, its software subsidiary Cariad took over the push.

Source: [TechCrunch: Argo AI shuts down (2022-10-26)](https://techcrunch.com/2022/10/26/ford-vw-backed-argo-ai-is-shutting-down/)

## A joint venture cannot stand alone: Motional restructures

- Motional is a joint venture between Aptiv and Hyundai Motor: announced in 2019 and established in 2020, the two sides co-invested $4 billion and each held 50%, using the Hyundai IONIQ 5 as its robotaxi.
- **In January 2024**, Aptiv announced it would no longer allocate capital to Motional; **in May 2024** the equity restructuring was completed: Hyundai added about $1 billion (direct capital injection + acquiring part of Aptiv's equity), Aptiv's common shares fell to 15%, and Hyundai obtained control.
- The restructuring was accompanied by layoffs of about 550 people (about 40%), commercial operations in Las Vegas via Uber/Lyft were suspended, and the robotaxi commercial launch date was postponed; the CEO stepped down in September 2024. The joint venture did not die, but it changed from "two giants building together" to "Hyundai transfusing blood alone".

Sources: [Aptiv official: Motional ownership restructuring completed (2024-05)](https://www.aptiv.com/en/newsroom/article/aptiv-and-hyundai-complete-motional-ownership-restructuring); [TechCrunch: layoff report (2024-05)](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/14/motional-cut-about-550-employees-around-40-in-recent-restructuring-sources-say/)

## Control case: Why Zoox lived to commercialization after being acquired

- **In June 2020**, Amazon acquired Zoox in full, at a price reported to be over $1.2 billion (Amazon did not officially disclose it), markedly below its valuation of about $3.2 billion in 2018 — the acquisition price was also discounted.
- The difference was in what followed: Zoox kept operating as an independent subsidiary, its management stayed on, and Amazon gave it long-term capital to burn cash at its own pace. **On September 10, 2025**, Zoox officially launched its robotaxi service in Las Vegas (free at first), using its in-house purpose-built vehicle with no steering wheel; in the same year it obtained an NHTSA exemption for its vehicles without traditional controls, its Hayward plant started production, and its target was an annual output of 10,000 units.
- It was likewise a "sale", and the teams of ATG, Level 5, and Zoox were all taken over as a whole — the difference lies in whether the buyer treats them as assets to integrate, or as a long-term undertaking to keep supporting.

Source: [Zoox Las Vegas launch report (2025-09)](https://techxplore.com/news/2025-09-amazon-zoox-robotaxi-las-vegas.pdf)

## Summary: A checklist of failure patterns

1. **Safety culture collapses before the technology**: in the Tempe crash and the Cruise incident, what crushed the projects was not a single technical failure, but the organizational response after the failure (failed oversight, distorted reporting). See in detail [[companies/cruise|The Cruise Note]] and [[regulation/safety-evidence|How to Read Safety Data]].
2. **Cash burn outruns the owner's patience**: ride-hailing companies (Uber, Lyft) cut L4 first under earnings pressure; the automaker (Ford) turned to L2/L3 after a $2.7 billion impairment.
3. **A two-shareholder joint venture has no final payer**: Argo shut down when it could not raise new external money; Motional triggered a restructuring when one side withdrew funding — a joint-venture structure shares costs in fair weather and amplifies deadlock in headwinds.
4. **Missed timetables consume the trust budget**: after commercialization was postponed again and again, capital turned to L2/L3 and assisted driving that could be monetized in the near term.
5. **The exit method decides the team's fate**: being taken over as a whole by a buyer with long-term capital (Aurora, Woven, Zoox) is the best outcome; a direct shutdown (Argo) is the worst; being absorbed by the parent company and transformed (Cruise) falls in between.
